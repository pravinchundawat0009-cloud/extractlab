# Day 2 — the extraction, hour by hour

**You finished Day 1 with a clear amber solvent and a measured density. Today you put leaf in it.**

Today is mostly waiting. You are actively working for about 25 minutes, then the solvent sits in a
45 °C bath for 3 hours doing the extraction while you do something else, then you spend about 60
minutes separating and labelling.

**Do not start today unless** your Day 1 solvent is clear and still clear this morning. If there are
crystals in it, stop and read the last page of this before you do anything else.

## Before the bath — three things must be right

Everything below is quick, but do not skip any of them. These three are the errors that make a
sample unexplainable later.

### 1. The bath must actually be at 45 °C before the containers go in

Switch it on **30 minutes early**. A bath set to 45 that is still at 38 will quietly shift your whole
extraction. Read the **thermometer in the water**, not the dial.

You should see: 45 ± 1 °C, stable. If it overshoots to 50 or drops to 40, fix it before loading.

### 2. The leaf mass must be weighed, not estimated

Weigh **0.500 g** into each of **M1**, **M2**, **M3**. Weigh each one separately and write down each
actual mass — they will not all be exactly 0.500.

You should see: half a gram in each. The fourth container, **BLANK**, stays empty — no leaf, ever.

**Why this is worth slowing down for:** this mass is the number your final result is divided by. Being
off by 0.04 g is an 8 % error in your answer, and you would never know it happened. Use the most
precise balance in the lab for this step, not the rough one used for the 143 g.

### 3. All four containers must have the same lid

Same type, same tightness, on all four. Not "similar" — the same.

**Why:** over 3 hours a loose lid lets water evaporate. Different lids mean your samples had different
water contents, and later you would blame the leaf for scatter that was really the lids. If you only
have three of one lid type, tell your supervisor before starting rather than mixing types.

## Loading — the 15 minutes that matter

### Step 1 — Weigh the solvent in. Do not pipette it.

Put the container on the balance, tare, and pour in **your calculated mass** (e.g. 36.0 g at
1.20 g/mL). Do it for all four.

**Weigh it because this liquid is thick like honey.** A pipetted "30 mL" can easily be 26 mL, because
so much stays on the inside of the pipette. That would silently change your solid-to-liquid ratio —
the exact kind of hidden error this experiment is designed to avoid. Weighing takes half the time and
is actually correct.

Record the mass you put in each of the four. These numbers go in the report.

You should see: a thick amber liquid sitting on top of the leaf powder.

### Step 2 — Stir each container for about 10 seconds with its own glass rod

Just enough to break the crust and wet the powder.

You should see: the leaf powder mixes in, rather than sitting dry on the surface.

**Why:** dry powder dropped into a 2000-times-thicker-than-water solvent forms a skin that never
wets properly. Ten seconds now prevents a sample that extracted badly for a reason nobody recorded.

**Use a separate rod for each, or wipe the rod between containers.** A rod carrying leaf from M1 into
the blank would contaminate the one sample whose whole job is to contain no leaf.

### Step 3 — Lid all four and photograph them

Same lid on each. Then take a photo of the four containers side by side, on the bench, even lighting.

You should see: three containers with green-brown powder and thick amber liquid, one clear amber
container with nothing in it.

**Why photograph:** you will take the same photo in 3 hours. The colour change between the two photos
is real data — it shows the extraction happened, and it shows whether the blank behaved the same way
as the samples. It costs 5 seconds.

## In the bath — the 3 hours

### Step 4 — Put all four in together and write the time

All four at once, so they all get the same duration.

**Write the start time here: ______**

### Step 5 — Then leave them alone

**No shaking. No stirring. No ultrasound. Do not lift them out to look.**

That stillness is the entire point of this experiment. The published work used 30 minutes of vigorous
stirring or 5 minutes of ultrasound; you are deliberately doing neither, to find out what plain
45 °C soaking achieves. Every time you move a container, you are quietly joining the experiment you
are trying not to run.

### Step 6 — At 1 hour: read the thermometer only

Look at the thermometer. Do not touch the containers.

**Bath temperature at 1 hour: ______ °C**

If it has dropped below 43: nudge the setting up, but note it — the containers will have run slightly
cool for part of the time, and that belongs in the report. Do not add more than a degree or two at a
time; a bath chasing a big change overshoots.

### Step 7 — At 3 hours: out, and read the temperature again

**Bath temperature at 3 hours: ______ °C**

**Write the end time: ______**

Take the photo now, same four containers, same angle as before.

## While you wait — use the 3 hours

Three useful things, about 15 minutes total.

### Finish the moisture calculation

From yesterday: you weighed the dish empty, then with leaf, then after 2 hours at 105 °C. Now:

```
moisture % = (dish + leaf before) − (dish + leaf after)
             ─────────────────────────────────────────  × 100
             (dish + leaf before) − (empty dish)
```

Worked, with example numbers:

```
empty dish         22.134 g
dish + leaf        23.158 g   → leaf = 1.024 g
after 2 h at 105 °C 23.041 g   → water lost = 0.117 g
moisture = 0.117 ÷ 1.024 × 100 = 11.4 %
```

**My moisture: ______ %**

5–12 % is completely normal for dried leaf. Above 15 %, mention it to your supervisor — that much
water entering a 20 wt % solvent matters.

### Prepare and label your tubes

Get out four clean tubes or vials. Write on each one, with a marker that survives the freezer:

- the container name: **M1**, **M2**, **M3**, **BLANK**
- the date
- **20%W 45C 3h** (the condition — useful when these are sitting in a freezer next to other people's
  samples three weeks from now)

Label them **before** you have liquid in your hands. It is much harder to label a tube you are
already holding.

So you know what to ask for while the lab is still open, rather than remembering at 5 p.m.

## Out of the bath — cooling and separation

### Step 8 — Let all four cool to room temperature before opening

**Do not open them while they are still warm.**

**Why:** warm liquid plus room air pulls in condensation, and then you have water in your sample that
you cannot account for in your volumes. Twenty minutes of patience prevents a mystery.

Once cool, you should see: the leaf powder settled at the bottom, and the liquid much darker than it
was this morning — especially in M1–M3 compared with the blank.

### Step 9 — Separate all four the same way

Use your lab's procedure, but **the identical procedure for all four containers**. If you centrifuge,
centrifuge all four at the same setting. If you filter, filter all four through the same material.

**Centrifuge: 20 000 × g for 30 minutes** if you have it — that is the published condition.

If your lab does not have an ultracentrifuge: use whatever your lab normally uses, at a setting that
gives you a firm pellet or a clear filtrate, and **write down exactly what you used.** "Whatever the
lab does" in the report is not reproducible; "3000 × g, 15 min, benchtop" is.

You should see: a solid pellet or a residue on the filter, and a clearer dark liquid above or through.

**If it will not filter:** switch to centrifugation rather than forcing it. Thick solvent plus fine
powder clogs filters, and forcing it through introduces air and evaporation.

### Step 10 — Decant into your labelled tubes and record the volumes

Careful, slow, without disturbing the pellet. One tube per container.

**Volume recovered:**

| | Volume |
|---|---:|
| M1 | ______ mL |
| M2 | ______ mL |
| M3 | ______ mL |
| BLANK | ______ mL |

**It will not be exactly 30 mL, and that is fine.** Some liquid stays in the pellet. Record what you
got. What you must not do is top it up with fresh solvent — that would dilute your sample with
solvent that never saw the leaf, and there is no way to correct for it afterwards.

## Freezer and handover

### Step 11 — Freeze all four at −20 °C, in the dark if you can

Label the date. Note how long they sit there before the HPLC runs them — that belongs in the report.

### Step 12 — Give the HPLC lab four things, in writing

Copy this into an email or a note. Do not rely on telling someone verbally.

> **Samples:** four, labelled M1, M2, M3, BLANK. Deep-eutectic solvent extracts of dried mango leaf
> (β-alanine : citric acid : water, 1:1 molar, 20 wt % water), 0.5 g leaf in 30 mL, 45 °C, 3 hours,
> static. The blank contains **no leaf** — it is solvent carried through the identical procedure.
>
> **Please:**
> 1. Run the blank **at the same dilution** as the samples. It is how we show the mangiferin came
>    from the leaf and not from the solvent.
> 2. Clean up before injection: dilute 1:4 with water, load onto a 1 g C18 SPE cartridge, wash with
>    4 mL water, elute with 3 mL methanol + 0.1 % formic acid. **Please do not inject the raw
>    solvent** — it is very viscous and will foul the column.
> 3. Detect at **254 nm**, C18 column; mangiferin is the peak we are quantifying. A mangiferin
>    reference standard is needed for calibration.
> 4. Tell me your detection limit and calibration range, so I know how much to dilute next time.

**Rough expectation, so you can sanity-check whatever comes back:** if the leaf is about 2 %
mangiferin and you recover half of it, the diluted solution should read roughly **40–70 µg/mL**. If
they report under 5 µg/mL, suspect the dilution or the SPE recovery before you suspect the leaf.

## If something goes wrong on Day 2

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Crystals in the solvent **before** you start | 20 wt % water was too dry for this mixture | Do not proceed. Label it and tell your supervisor. This is a finding worth reporting, not a failure to hide. |
| Two layers in the solvent before you start | The water separated | Do not use it. Tell your supervisor. |
| Leaf will not wet in, sits on top | Added too fast, or not stirred | Stir longer, 30 seconds. It will wet. |
| Bath dropped to 40 °C or below | Containers went in cold, or bath undersized | Note the temperature and time. Do not chase it with a big dial change. |
| Something looks different in M2 only | Uneven wetting or a different lid | Record it now, in writing. Do not adjust the others to match. |
| Will not filter | Viscous solvent, fine powder | Centrifuge instead — same for all four. |
| Pellet disturbed while decanting | Careless decant | Note it. Do not re-centrifuge "to fix it" and then pretend the volume was the same. |
| One tube much lighter in colour than the others | Real variability, or a lid leaked | Record it. Check the container masses — a big mass loss points at the lid. |
| Recovered far less than 30 mL | Normal, some stays with the pellet | Record the real volume. **Never top up with fresh solvent.** |

## What Day 2 should leave you with

By the end of today you should have **four labelled tubes in a freezer** and a page of numbers.

You will not have an answer yet — that needs the HPLC. But if you have done today properly, you will
be able to say all of this:

- how much leaf was in each of the three samples, to the milligram
- how much solvent went into each of the four containers, by weight not by volume
- what the bath was actually at, at the start, middle and end
- what the moisture of the leaf was
- how much liquid you actually recovered from each
- and a photo showing the colour before and after

That is a complete, defensible record. If the numbers that come back later look strange, those six
things are what let you work out *why* — instead of guessing.

**The two rules that matter most today:** weigh the solvent, do not pipette it — honey-thick liquids do
not measure by volume. And put all four in the bath, then leave them alone: the stillness is the
experiment.
